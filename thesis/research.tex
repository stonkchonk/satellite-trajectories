	\begin{abstract}
		\centering
		This paper aims to determine how well image-based approaches are able to predict satellite trajectories from short observation spans. Four orbit determination methods are presented: two based on a single-camera and two on a dual-camera setup, with one method in each setup determining circular orbits and the other full orbital parameters. The primary experimental setup includes the placement of nine observer cameras in specific locations and variable observation spans. A secondary experimental setup examines the influence of star-based view vector determination on orbit prediction accuracy. Another secondary setup tests a self developed method's ability to determine satellite orbits at different altitudes. Results indicate that methods for full orbit determination predict orbits reasonably well under the right circumstances. However, predicted trajectories are unsuitable for high-precision applications. Methods for circular orbit determination yield more accurate results. Furthermore, we find that there is generally no correlation between view vector determination and orbit prediction, implying that the noise arises from another source. Lastly, we find that the self developed method is indeed applicable to multiple satellite altitudes.\newline
		Analysis and series of measurements located here: \url{https://github.com/stonkchonk/satellite-trajectories}
	\end{abstract}

	\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
	Orbit determination of satellites in Low Earth Orbit is achievable by various means. Specifically ground-based methods require measurements spanning long arcs in the range of 3/4 to several resolutions, as stated by Carpenter et al. \cite{semi_major_axis_knowledge}. This research paper's primary aim is to determine, how well satellite trajectories may be predictable using an image-based approach taking measurements from a single observable flyover, thus short arcs. Hereby, we aim to compare single- and dual-camera setups with fixed camera properties.\newline
	This includes a self developed method for circular orbit determination which is based on the initial idea of the Gaussian algorithm for orbit determination. The paper's secondary objective is to determine how susceptible this method is to inaccuracies of star-based view vector determination. Lastly, we aim to determine, whether this new method can predict satellite orbits with differing semi major axes, spanning various altitudes in the Low Earth Orbit region.
	
	\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
	\subsection{Single-camera orbit determination}\label{sec:one_camera_determination}
	\subsubsection{Gaussian method}\label{sec:gauss_method}
	The Gaussian method's approach is to numerically fit observations to a physically feasible orbit.
	The algorithm yields parameters for full orbit characterization, namely semi major axis $a$, eccentricity $e$, argument of periapsis $\omega$ and plane normal vector $n$.\newline A single observer camera is required, whereas three measurements, each consisting of position vector $p_i$, view vector $v_i$ and corresponding time stamp $t_i$ need to be recorded to determine a solution. \cite{curtis_gauss_algo}
	\subsubsection{Simplified Gaussian method}\label{sec:simplified_gauss_method}
	The simplified Gaussian method is a self developed approach to numerically fit observations to a physically feasibly circular orbit. Its incentive stems from the condition that satellite orbits low above earth's surface possess very low eccentricities and can mostly be considered circular. The algorithm yields parameters for circle-orbit characterization, namely semi major axis $a$ and plane normal vector $n$. \newline Similar to the Gaussian method, a single observer camera is required, whereas only two measurements, each consisting of position vector $p_i$, view vector $v_i$ and corresponding time stamp $t_i$, need to be recorded to determine a solution. Hereby, the solution consists of two scalar parameters $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ which must satisfy the radius equality condition as well as the arc of a circle condition stated as follows:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\label{circle_gauss_circle_condition}
		|r_1|=|r_2|\Leftrightarrow \sqrt{(p_1+\rho_1v_1)^2}=\sqrt{(p_2+\rho_2v_2)^2}
	\end{equation}

	
	\begin{equation}
		\label{circle_gauss_time_condition}
		\begin{aligned}
			&\arccos\left(\frac{(p_1+\rho_1v_1)\cdot (p_2+\rho_2v_2)}{|(p_1+\rho_1v_1)|^2}\right)\cdot|(p_1+\rho_1v_1)|^{\frac{3}{2}}=\\
			&(t_2-t_1)\sqrt{\mu}
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	\cite{orbit_determination_preliminary}
	
	\subsection{Dual-camera orbit determination}\label{sec:two_camera_determination}
	\subsubsection{Full parametric trajectory}\label{sec:full_parametric_trajectory}
	An orbital trajectory can be described as parametric curve lying in its orbital plane as follows: 
	\begin{equation}
		r(\theta)=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cdot\cos(\theta-\omega)} \cdot \begin{pmatrix}
			\cos(\theta) \\
			\sin(\theta)
		\end{pmatrix}
	\end{equation}
	\noindent
	Given three positions in polar coordinates defined by radii $r_i$ and arguments $\theta_i$, the orbital properties $a$, $e$ and $\omega$ for eccentricities $e \notin \{0,1\}$ are defined by:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\omega=\alpha_{13}-\arctan(G)
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
		e=\frac{r_1-r_2}{C_{12}\cdot\cos(\alpha_{12}-\omega)}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}
		a=\frac{r_1(1+e\cdot\cos(\theta_1-\omega))}{1-e^2}
	\end{equation}
	\noindent
	Hereby, $G$, $C_{12}$, $\alpha_{12}$ and $\alpha_{13}$ represent terms consisting of $r_1$, $r_2$, $r_3$, $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ and $\theta_3$.\newline
	Two observer cameras are required, utilizing means of stereo vision. Three position vectors of the satellite are obtained by three synchronous measurements which yield values for radii $r_i$, arguments $\theta_i$ and the plane normal vector $n$. \cite{orbit_determination_preliminary}

	\subsubsection{Circular trajectory}\label{sec:circular_parametric_trajectory}
	This method once again simplifies the orbit to a circular trajectory. Two observer cameras are required, utilizing means of stereo vision. Two position vectors of the satellite are obtained by two synchronous measurements which yield the semi major axis $a$ and the plane normal vector $n$. \cite{orbit_determination_preliminary}

	\subsubsection{Influence of star-based \\ view vector determination}
	As elaborated in the preliminary text, view vector determination relies three known stars or rather their view vectors $v_{S1}$, $v_{S2}$ and $v_{S3}$ to form a matrix $M$ for triangulation \cite{orbit_determination_preliminary} which is more or less suited for view vector determination through triangulation. One metric to measure suitability is the determinant $d$, as it indicates linear dependency of its row/column vectors formulated as follows:
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:determinant}
		d = \det(M)
	\end{equation} 
	\noindent
	Another useful metric is the condition number $\kappa(M)$ which indicates how sensitive the triangulation matrix $M$ is to errors formulated as follows:
	\begin{equation}
		\begin{aligned}
			\kappa(M) = \left\lVert M \right\rVert_2 \cdot \left\lVert M^{-1}\right\rVert_2 \text{with}\quad \left\lVert M \right\rVert_2 = \max_k \sigma_k
		\end{aligned}
	\end{equation}
	\noindent
	Hereby, the 2-norm of matrix $M$ is defined by its maximum singular value $\sigma_k$ \cite{condition_numbers}.\newline
	We hypothesize that there might be a correlation between the accuracy of a predicted orbit and the suitability of matrix $M$ used for view vector determination. For testing purposes, we perform orbit determination of the ground truth orbit,  defined in table \ref{tab:ground_truth_orbit}, using distinct sets of stars for view vector calibration. Observations are conducted by one camera placed at $L_5$ starting at certain predefined time stamps so that numerous background stars are visible to form many distinctive 
	sets of stars for calibration. The determination algorithm of choice is the simplified Gaussian method with a fixed observation span $\Delta t = 7s$.

	\subsection{Metrics}
	\subsubsection{Parameter stability}\label{sec:param_stability_definition}
	All presented orbit determination methods yield values for semi major axis $a$ and plane normal vector $n$. Additionally, methods not assuming a circular orbit, yield values for eccentricity $e$ and argument of periapsis $\omega$. The goal is to compare measured (denoted $m$) with ground truth (denoted $g$) properties in a generalized form independent of units. For this reason, we define error metrics $f_a$, $f_e$, $f_{\omega}$ and $f_n$ for $a$, $e$, $\omega$ and $n$ as follows::\newline
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:error_sma}
		f_a=\frac{|a_g-a_m|}{a_g}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:error_ecc}
		f_e=|e_g-e_m|
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:error_omega}
		f_\omega=|\omega_g-\omega_m|
	\end{equation}
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:error_n}
		f_n=\arccos\left(\frac{n_g\cdot n_m}{|n_g|\cdot|n_m|}\right)=\arccos(n_g\cdot n_m) \\
	\end{equation}
	Hereby, specifically for metric \ref{eq:error_omega}, it must be noted that this expression is undefined if ground truth or measured orbit has an eccentricity of $e=0$.
	
	\subsubsection{Propagation error}\label{sec:error_propagation_definition}
	The determined orbit of a satellite differs from the actual ground truth by a margin of error. As the satellite continues orbiting Earth, actual and predicted position continue drifting apart due to this error. Carpenter et al. state that the position error along the trajectory arises from the semi major axis error. This formulates as an equation for the variation in orbital period $\delta T$ subject to variation in semi major axis $\delta a$ as:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\delta T = 3\pi\sqrt{\frac{a}{\mu}}\cdot\delta a = 3\pi\sqrt{\frac{a}{\mu}}\cdot|a_g-a_m|
	\end{equation}
	\noindent
	\cite{semi_major_axis_knowledge} \newline
	Our approach is to formulate the position or rather propagation error in such a way that factors in all orbital parameters and that it can be described as distance metric between the satellite ground truth position $s_g$ and the measured or predicted satellite position $s_m$.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{images/drift.png}
		\caption{Schematic of propagating error between ground truth and predicted position.}
		\label{fig:error_propagation_schematic}
	\end{figure}
	\noindent
	The schematic figure above illustrates how the propagation error accumulates over the course of a single ground truth orbit. The location vector of a satellite can be described subjected to propagated true anomaly $\theta$ or passed time $t$ or number of orbital revolutions $m$ since measurement time $t_0$. Thereby, we define the propagation error as distance between ground truth and predicted position in relation to the ground truth semi major axis $a_g$ subject to orbital revolutions $m$ since $t_0$ as follows:
	
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:propagation_error}
		f_p = \frac{|s_g(m) - s_m(m)|}{a_g}
	\end{equation}
	
	\section{Results}\label{sec:results_discussion}
	In the following, we evaluate error metrics $f_a$, $f_e$, $f_n$ and $f_p$ subject to different properties for defined experimental setups. Regarding propagation error $f_p$, it must be noted that this value is only calculated for plausible predicted orbits. If the predicted orbit has an eccentricity of $e\geq1$ or has a periapsis below 150km above Earth's surface, it is considered implausible, resulting in the corresponding tile intentionally left blank as visible in figures \ref{fig:propagation_errors_full_single} and \ref{fig:propagation_errors_full_dual}. \newline 
	Stereo vision models require measurements from two observer locations denoted as $L_iLj$ with an angle $\delta$ which describes the mean intersection angle between lines of sight for all synchronous measurements from $t_0$ to $t_n$. \newline
	Lastly, it must be noted that no error $f_e$ is determined for circular ground truth to circular model comparison, as it is always 0 in that case. Furthermore, error $f_\omega$ is not determined for any experiment, as it is undefined due to circular ground truth orbits.
	
	\subsection{Variable observation spans and \\ observer placement}\label{sec:variability_result}
	\textbf{Gaussian method:} \newline
	Figures \ref{fig:sma_errors_full_single}, \ref{fig:ecc_errors_full_single} and \ref{fig:pln_errors_full_single} show error values $f_a$, $f_e$ and $f_n$ subject to single observer location $L_i$ and angular passage $\Delta \theta$. Hereby, prediction stability seems to be dependent on observer location and angular passage. We observe that errors decrease with growing $\Delta \theta$ further from $L_z$. At $\Delta \theta > 25^\circ$ and ideal observer location, the magnitude of $f_a$ equates to roughly $10^{-2}a_g \;\widehat{=}\; 71km$. At $L_z$, errors remain persistently high, indicating a numerical instability of the Gaussian method. Plane normal angle deviation seems to converge in broader areas to magnitudes of roughly $f_n=0.2^\circ$.\newline
	The error propagation value $f_p$ for propagated orbits $m=1$ behaves in a similar manner, as pictured in figure \ref{fig:propagation_errors_full_single}. Excluding a few individual tiles, the magnitude of $f_p$ in ideal situations equates to roughly $0.1a_g \;\widehat{=}\; 707km$.
	\newline\newline
	\textbf{Stereo vision (full parametric model):}\newline
	Figures \ref{fig:sma_errors_full_dual}, \ref{fig:ecc_errors_full_dual} and \ref{fig:pln_errors_full_dual} show  error values $f_a$, $f_e$ and $f_n$ subject to intersection angle $\delta$ and angular passage $\Delta \theta$. Once again, prediction stability seems to be dependent on both properties, so that accuracy increases with larger $\delta$ and $\Delta \theta$. At $\Delta \theta > 25^\circ$ and $\delta_{max}=25.96^\circ$ the magnitude of $f_a$ equates to roughly $10^{-2.4}a_g \;\widehat{=}\; 28km$. Plane normal angle deviation converges in broader areas to magnitudes of roughly $f_n=0.1^\circ$. \newline
	Lastly, $f_p$ values as pictured in figure \ref{fig:propagation_errors_full_dual} contain less plausible tiles. However, under optimal conditions $f_p$ equates to roughly $0.02a_g \;\widehat{=}\; 141km$. Overall, this method yields slightly more accurate results than the Gaussian method.
	\newline\newline
	\textbf{Simplified Gaussian method:}\newline
	Figures \ref{fig:sma_errors_circle_single}, \ref{fig:pln_errors_circle_single} and \ref{fig:propagation_errors_circle_single} show error values $f_a$, $f_n$ and $f_p$ subject to observer location and angular passage. Error values seem to converge at $\Delta \theta \geq 0.18^\circ$ which equates to an observation span of just $\Delta t \geq 3s$. There does not seem to be an accuracy pattern caused by observer location, rather differences seem to be caused by noise, meaning that location has no notable effect on results. Ideally, error $f_a$ equates to roughly $10^{-4}a_g \;\widehat{=}\; 0.7km$, plane normal deviates by $f_n=0.1^\circ$ and $f_p < 10^{-2}a_g \;\widehat{=}\; 71km$. As there is no pattern subject to observer location, these ideal results can not be assumed in every case.\newline
	Overall, this method is more accurate than the Gaussian and stereo vision method for full parametric trajectories in determining properties of circular orbits. A particular advantage over the classical Gaussian method is the aspect that there is no numerical instability at $L_z$ and other close locations.
	\newline\newline
	\textbf{Stereo vision (circular model):}\newline
	Figures \ref{fig:sma_errors_circle_dual}, \ref{fig:pln_errors_circle_dual} and \ref{fig:propagation_errors_circle_dual} show error values $f_a$, $f_n$ and $f_p$. All three error values decrease with increased intersection angle $\delta$. No change in magnitude can be observed by altering $\Delta \theta$.\newline
	Overall, this method is more accurate than the Gaussian and stereo vision method for full parametric trajectories in determining properties of circular orbits. Compared to the simplified Gaussian method, error values are less susceptible to noise. A particular disadvantage is the aspect, that a second camera is needed which needs to be placed at substantial distance from the first one.
	
	\subsection{Influence of star-based \\ view vector determination}\label{sec:star_calib_result}
	The Gaussian, stereo vision for full parametric trajectories and simplified Gaussian methods yield noisy error results, hypothesized to originate from star-based view vector determination. Hence, we observe propagation error $f_p$ subject to determinant or condition number and $m$ propagated circulations. Figures \ref{fig:propagation_error_condition_number} and \ref{fig:propagation_error_determinant} illustrate these relations where the same orbit is predicted exemplary by the simplified Gaussian method, calibrating view vectors on 153 distinct sets of three stars.\newline
	We observe that for a wide range of determinants or conditions numbers, $f_p$ remains relatively low for the first few $m$ circulations. As $m$ increases, $f_p$ increases for some calibrations but not for all. There does not seem to be a general clear correlation as $f_p$ for larger $m$ may be low for less favorable determinants or condition numbers.\newline
	A steep increase of $f_p$ is only observable in extreme cases $\kappa > 1110$ and $d < 0.000387$.
	
	\subsection{Influence of variable semi major axis}\label{sec:result_influence_sma}
	Figure \ref{fig:propagation_error_variable_sma} shows error $f_p$ subject to observation span $\Delta t$ and semi major axis $a$ for the simplified Gaussian method.\newline
	We observe that it is possible to obtain propagation errors $f_p < 10^{-2}a_g$. This error is sometimes larger but does not appear to dependent on $a$, rather a result of noise.\newline
	This proves that the self developed simplified Gaussian method can be applied to satellites in a variety of altitudes in the Low Earth Orbit region.

	\section{Discussion and outlook}\label{sec:conclusion}
	The primary goal of this research was to determine how well satellite trajectories are determinable using image-based methods and short observation arcs. Secondary goals consisted of identifying, whether errors in star-based view vector determination have an effect on accuracy and testing if a self developed algorithm for circular orbit determination works for different satellite altitudes.\newline
	As for the primary goal, all presented methods were able to predict a satellite's orbit with varying accuracy. The Gaussian and the stereo vision method for full parametric trajectory determination are able to yield reasonably accurate results, for an observation span equating 1/10 of an arc if observers were placed favorably. However, these approaches are unsuitable for high-precision applications as predicted position drift from ground truth steeply, resulting in offsets of tens to hundreds of kilometers after just one circulation.\newline
	The simplified Gaussian as well as the stereo vision method for circular trajectory determination are able to yield more accurate results, specifically in terms of parameter accuracy. Especially noteworthy is their required observation span to determine stable parameters which is less than 1/1000 of an arc. The drift between predicted position and ground truth is offset by tens of kilometers after one orbit, so these might be suitable for certain applications.\newline
	One aspect that has not been considered in this paper are non-circular ground truth orbits. They may be predictable by the Gaussian and stereo vision method for full parametric trajectory determination, but further research would need to be conducted to determine whether results are of similar accuracy. Models for circular determination would generally be unsuitable for this task, whereby the resulting error between elliptical ground truth and circular prediction may also be of research interest.\newline
	As for the secondary objective, we hypothesized that instabilities in star-based view vector determination might yield inaccurate orbital predictions. Results show, that there seems to be no obvious correlation between accuracy and determinant or condition number of the star calibration matrix for general cases. This means that there must be other sources of noise to be investigated. In this context, it is also noteworthy to state that \emph{Space Engine} is a proprietary black box. Usually, this simulation is quite a good representation of space, however there might be unknown rendering effects which are not accounted for. A future approach would be to verify, whether predicted accuracies can be replicated in other simulations or even in real life.
